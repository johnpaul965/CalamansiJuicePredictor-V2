2.1 Comparative Regression Frameworks for Citrus Yield Modeling

In agricultural yield estimation, regression algorithms provide the mathematical foundation for converting physical fruit metrics into volumetric output. This study implements and compares four distinct regression models:

1. Simple Linear Regression (SLR): Serving as the benchmark baseline, SLR models a direct proportional relationship between fruit mass (W) and juice yield (Y). While computationally lightweight, it assumes a constant extraction ratio across all fruit sizes, which overlooks non-linear fruit rind-to-pulp ratios (Montgomery et al., 2021).

2. Polynomial Regression (Degree 2): To capture curvature in juice yield, second-degree polynomial regression introduces a quadratic term (W2). This accounts for the rate of change in internal juice sac volume as fruit mass expands, though unconstrained polynomial curves risk extreme extrapolation errors at outer weight boundaries (James et al., 2021).

3. Support Vector Regression (SVR with RBF Kernel): Developed by Vapnik (1995), SVR applies structural risk minimization using an ε-insensitive loss function, ignoring prediction errors smaller than ε = 0.1 mL. Paired with the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, SVR maps non-linear fruit weight distributions into high-dimensional space without requiring an explicit mathematical curve, offering robust resistance to local data fluctuations (Smola & Schölkopf, 2004; Awad & Khanna, 2015).

4. Proposed Champion Model: Polynomial (d=2) + Natural Logarithm with L2 Ridge Regularization (α = 0.01): Combining quadratic expansion (W, W2) with an allometric natural logarithmic term (ln W) allows the model to mirror true biological growth, where small fruits exhibit disproportionately thicker rinds relative to pulp volume (Huxley, 1932). To prevent multicollinearity and runaway variance between W, W2, and ln(W), L2 Ridge regularization introduces a shrinkage penalty (α ∑ βj2) that stabilizes coefficient weights (Hoerl & Kennard, 1970; Hastie et al., 2009).

2.2 Rigorous Two-Stage Outlier Screening in Agricultural Datasets

Raw empirical datasets gathered under field or market conditions frequently contain human measurement errors and biological anomalies. Tabachnick and Fidell (2019) emphasize that uncurated outliers distort least-squares regression lines, resulting in biased slope estimates and deflated R2 values. To ensure dataset integrity without arbitrary trimming, this study established a two-stage screening protocol:

1. Physiological Feasibility Screening (Stage 1): Due to the physical presence of rind, pulp membranes, and seeds, the liquid juice recovery of mature calamansi never exceeds 50% to 55% of total fruit mass under natural conditions. Samples presenting an extraction ratio Juice (mL) / Weight (g) > 0.60 violate biological limits and reflect manual scale-tare or graduated-cylinder transcription typos, justifying immediate removal.

2. Standardized Z-Score Screening per Gram Group (Stage 2): Because fruit juice variance naturally increases with mass (heteroscedasticity), global outlier cutoffs fail. Instead, observations are partitioned into 1-gram integer weight bins (2 g to 21 g). Within each bin, local mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) are calculated. Observations exceeding |Z| > 2.326—corresponding to the 99% two-tailed confidence interval (α = 0.01)—are screened out as atypical specimens (Barnett & Lewis, 1994). This objective method retained 97.11% of gathered data (N = 1,478) while purging true distortions.
